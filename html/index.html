BAY 01

Pipeline

Five counts, two engines, one sum

Five counts

Burnside's lemma counts orbits as the average number of elements each group element fixes. The eight symmetries of the square act on the fixed hole-free n-ominoes, and the orbits are the free ones, so A000104(n) = (F + R2 + 2 R4 + 2 M + 2 D) / 8.

F counts every fixed hole-free n-omino (A006724). R2, R4, M and D count those with a half-turn, a quarter-turn, a vertical mirror and a diagonal mirror symmetry. At n = 46 the largest of the four is about 3 × 10⁻¹³ of F.

TEX§2.2 Counting by symmetry › The Burnside formula

Two engines

A transfer matrix over self-avoiding polygons gives F, and a test at each polygon's center inside the same sweep gives R2. A Redelmeier search over the orbits of each symmetry gives R4, M and D, and R2 again as a check.

TEX§3 AlgorithmsRUSTsrc/tm.rs, src/search.rs, src/lib.rs

Exact from residues

The sweep counts in 64-bit words, once modulo 2⁶⁴ and once modulo the prime 2⁶¹ − 1, and the Chinese remainder theorem rebuilds each count from the two residues.

BAY 02

Symmetry

Uholey polyominoes, their images and their cores

Hole-free means one polygon

A polyomino has no holes exactly when its boundary is a single self-avoiding polygon. A pinch, a vertex where only two diagonally opposite cells are present, always encloses a hole: the path of cells joining the two, closed through the pinch, cuts off one of the empty cells there.

So F(n) is A006724, the self-avoiding polygons of area n, and the transfer matrix can count polygons in place of polyominoes.

TEX§2.3 Counting by symmetry › Uholey polyominoes are polygons

Holes by the Euler characteristic

For a connected union of closed cells, χ = V − E + F is 1 minus the number of holes, where V, E and F count its distinct corners, edges and cells. The search keeps χ as it adds cells.

TEX§3.3 Algorithms › The search for the symmetric counts

Forced cores

A rotation that maps a hole-free polyomino onto itself has its center in the interior, so the one, two or four cells around the center belong to it. A mirror line meets it in one segment, so the cells on the axis form one unbroken run.

BAY 03

The cut

One polygon through the transfer matrix, vertex by vertex

The state

The polygon sits in a strip of W cell rows. The sweep visits vertices bottom to top within a column and columns left to right, and after each vertex the cut between visited and unvisited vertices crosses W + 2 edges.

For each crossed edge the state holds 0 if the polygon does not use it, and 1 or 2 if it carries the lower or upper end of an arc drawn so far. Arcs cannot cross, so the 1s and 2s nest like parentheses. A state is a 64-bit word, 2 bits per edge.

TEX§3.1 Algorithms › The transfer matrix for F · after Enting (1980), Sects. 4 and 5

The step at a vertex

A vertex uses 0 or 2 of its 4 edges. Two are crossed before it is visited, the edge from below and the edge from the left, and two after, so the step starts an arc, extends one, or joins two ends. Joining two lower ends turns the partner of the upper one into a lower end, and joining two upper ends does the reverse. Joining the two ends of one arc closes the polygon, allowed only when no other arc is open.

RUSTsrc/tm.rs · vertex(), partner_up(), partner_down()

Area by parity

A cell is inside when an odd number of used edges lies below it in its column, and the state gives that parity as the sweep passes the cell. Each state carries one count per area.

BAY 04

Sweep

Every state at once: layers, pruning, the mirror merge, shards

Layers and state-steps

The states at one point of the sweep form a layer. Each state is a word and a window of counts by area, and the unit of work is the state-step, one state processed at one vertex. State-steps and peak states are exact and the same on every machine.

TEX§4.1 The program › The sweep's states

Pruning

Every column of a polygon holds a cell, and the strip of W rows only needs polygons of length at least W. So after column c a state is dropped once its least area passes N − (W − 1 − c).

The mirror merge

The strip is symmetric top to bottom, so at a column boundary a state and its mirror image, positions reversed and labels 1 and 2 swapped, have the same completions. The two merge into one. At N = 24 this removes 44% of the states at column boundaries.

RUSTsrc/tm.rs · next_column(), mirror()TEX§3.1 · The mirror merge · after Barequet and Ben-Shachar (2026), Sect. 4.3

Shards and occupancy groups

A layer of 16,384 states or more splits into four shards per thread by a multiplicative hash of the word. A step runs in two passes: each shard maps its states to successors and routes them to their new shard, then each shard adds up what it received.

While the sweep works on one window of rows, states that differ in the occupancy outside the window never combine. Under a memory limit these groups go to bucket files on disk, and each bucket is stepped in memory.

BAY 05

Strips

From placements to exact heights by second differences

Each polygon once

A run in a strip of W rows counts a polygon of height h ≤ W once for each of its W − h + 1 vertical placements. With TW that strip's count at one area and length, the polygons of height exactly h number Th − 2 Th−1 + Th−2.

A quarter-turn swaps height and length L, so only h ≤ L is needed, counted twice when h < L. Since h + L − 1 ≤ N, the strips stop at ⌈N/2⌉ rows.

TEX§3.1 Algorithms › The transfer matrix for FRUSTsrc/tm.rs · fixed_with()

The difference stays exact under pruning

It is taken at each length L separately. For h ≤ L it needs strips h, h − 1 and h − 2 only at lengths L ≥ h, and the strip of W rows keeps every polygon of length at least W.

One run per N

A run to N truncates every count at area N, so it cannot later be extended to N + 1: each N chosen costs a full run, and a run gives every term up to N.

BAY 06

Half-turn

R2 from the same sweep, by a test at the center

Half a polygon is enough

A polygon with 180-degree symmetry is its left half plus the half-turned copy of that half. The sweep for F already carries every left half, since the copy is one possible completion, so R2 needs only a test on states the sweep already has, made where it reaches the center.

TEX§3.2 Algorithms › R2 from the same transfer matrix · Shirakawa (2025), Sect. 6.1 suggests this as future work

The test

A state passes if its occupancy, which edges are used, is symmetric under the half-turn (a bit reversal of the word), no used edge touches the center, and its arcs with their half-turned copies form a single loop. Only the few states that pass the bit reversal reach the loop walk.

RUSTsrc/tm.rs · glues(), record_r2()

Four centers

The center sits on the strip's middle line, at height W/2. Odd L puts it in a column of cells: a cell center for odd h, a horizontal edge's midpoint for even h, tested after the column's last vertex. Even L puts it on a vertex column: a vertex for even h, a vertical edge's midpoint for odd h, tested partway up the column.

The swept cells and their images overlap in the cells the cut splits, so the area is 2a − i (odd L) or 2a − 2i (even L).

BAY 07

Search

Redelmeier's recursion over orbits of a symmetry

Add or exclude

Redelmeier's algorithm lists every connected set of cells that contains a start set, each once. It keeps a list of untried neighbors; each untried cell is either added, and its new neighbors join the list, or excluded for the rest of that branch.

TEX§3.3 Algorithms › The search for the symmetric counts · Redelmeier (1981)

Orbits in place of cells

Run on the orbits of a symmetry g in place of single cells, from a forced core, the recursion lists the polyominoes that contain the core and are symmetric under g. An orbit adds 1, 2 or 4 cells, one at a time, to keep χ.

Adding a cell changes χ by its corners not yet present plus its neighbors already present, minus 3. A set is counted when χ = 1.

TEX§4.3 The program › The search · cores after Mason (2022)

Units

A run more than 11 orbits deep splits at depth 8 into 4d − 11 parts, at most 1,024; part i of p continues only every p-th branch. The split depends on the run alone, so the units are the same on every machine and thread count.

BAY 08

Residues

Counting on counters that wrap around, then rebuilding the count

The counts are too big for one counter

The sweep keeps its counts in 64-bit words. A word wraps around after 2⁶⁴ − 1, a 20-digit number, the way a car's odometer rolls over to 0. F(46) has 26 digits, so a single word cannot hold it.

Two counters are enough

The program runs the sweep twice, with counters that wrap at two different points: once at 2⁶⁴, once at 2⁶¹ − 1. Each run on its own loses the top of the number. Together they pin it down, because only one number below the two wrap points multiplied together leaves both readings.

The example on the right shows the same thing with counters that wrap at 100 and at 97.

Smaller counters, more runs

The sweep can also use 16-bit counters, which wrap near 65,536 and take about a third of the memory. Then it needs more runs, one counter each, until the wrap points multiplied together pass the largest the count can be.

BAY 09

Checks

Subgroup sums, data checks, independent pieces

Subgroup sums

Burnside's lemma holds for every subgroup of the symmetry group, and each subgroup's sum must be a multiple of its order. An error e in F or R2 escapes the full sum only when 8 divides e, and the subgroup sums narrow down which count is wrong.

TEX§5.3 Results › Checks

What the checks reach

Above n = 41 the checks that use none of the sweep's code reach F only modulo 8: R2 from the search checks the sweep's R2, and the Burnside sums catch an error in F only when 8 does not divide it.

Burnside sums, one per group of symmetries

Each group of the square's symmetries gives its own Burnside sum: add up, over the group's symmetries, how many fixed polyominoes each one fixes. The sum must divide evenly by the group's size, so each tile shows its remainder, which must be 0.
